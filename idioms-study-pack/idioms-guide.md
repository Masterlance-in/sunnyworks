# Ten English Idioms: Meaning, Origin, Example & Memory Hook

Each numbered item maps to the same-numbered panel on `idioms-illustrated-study-sheet.jpg` (or the full-resolution PNG). Hindi glosses are concise learning aids, not word-for-word translations.

## 1. A bad patch
- **Meaning:** A difficult period; a spell when things are going badly.
- **Hindi:** कठिनाई का दौर.
- **Origin note:** The exact origin of the idiomatic phrase is not firmly documented. *Patch* can mean a limited stretch or period; the expression pictures a rough stretch in an otherwise longer journey. Treat that as an explanation of the image, not a proven origin story.
- **Example:** The team went through a bad patch after several key players were injured.
- **Memory hook:** Imagine walking through one muddy patch on a long road: it is a hard phase, not the whole journey.

## 2. A beast of burden
- **Meaning:** An animal used to carry loads, such as a donkey, mule, or ox; figuratively, someone made to carry a heavy workload.
- **Hindi:** बोझ ढोने वाला पशु.
- **Origin note:** This is a literal descriptive expression, rather than an idiom with a single known inventor. The phrase is recorded in English from the eighteenth century; Etymonline dates *beast of burden* to about 1740. Dictionary.com gives a later first-recorded range (1795–1805), so the precise earliest date depends on the source.
- **Example:** The donkey acted as a beast of burden, carrying supplies up the hill.
- **Memory hook:** Picture the pack on the donkey's back: the animal bears the burden.

## 3. A beehive / a hive of activity
- **Meaning:** A very busy, crowded, energetic place.
- **Hindi:** बहुत व्यस्त जगह.
- **Origin note:** The metaphor is based on the activity of bees around a hive. Etymonline records *beehive* as a figurative term for a busy place from the 1610s. “A hive of activity” is a common form of the expression.
- **Example:** The market was a hive of activity before the festival.
- **Memory hook:** Imagine bees constantly coming and going; a busy place works the same way.

## 4. A big draw
- **Meaning:** A major attraction that brings many people.
- **Hindi:** बड़ा आकर्षण.
- **Origin note:** *Draw* is used in the sense of something that draws or attracts an audience. I did not find a well-supported, specific origin story for the complete phrase, so its precise coinage is uncertain.
- **Example:** The singer was the festival's biggest draw.
- **Memory hook:** Picture a crowd being drawn toward the main attraction.

## 5. A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush
- **Meaning:** A sure thing you already have may be wiser to keep than a chance at something better but uncertain.
- **Hindi:** निश्चित चीज़ को अनिश्चित बेहतर चीज़ के लिए जोखिम में न डालें.
- **Origin note:** This is an old proverb, with variants appearing in English by the fifteenth century. The familiar “two in the bush” form is recorded by John Ray in 1670. Falconry is sometimes offered as an explanation, but the proverb's documented history does not prove that this was its original source.
- **Example:** I kept my stable job while considering the uncertain offer—a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush.
- **Memory hook:** One bird safely in your hand is certain; the two in the bush may fly away.

## 6. A bird's-eye view
- **Meaning:** A view from high above; also, a broad overall view of a subject.
- **Hindi:** ऊपर से दिखने वाला दृश्य; समग्र दृष्टि.
- **Origin note:** The image is a bird looking down from above. The phrase's exact coinage is not established here; its meaning follows naturally from that elevated viewpoint.
- **Example:** This map gives us a bird's-eye view of the whole town.
- **Memory hook:** Imagine borrowing a bird's position in the sky to see the whole scene at once.

## 7. A bite at the cherry
- **Meaning:** A chance or opportunity to do something; often, another chance.
- **Hindi:** मौका मिलना.
- **Origin note:** This expression is especially associated with British English. The image suggests getting a turn at something desirable, but a dependable specific historical origin was not established in the sources checked; avoid presenting a speculative story as fact.
- **Example:** She missed the first selection, but got another bite at the cherry the following year.
- **Memory hook:** Picture reaching for a ripe cherry: getting a bite means getting your opportunity.

## 8. A blessing in disguise (also: a boon in disguise)
- **Meaning:** Something that first seems unfortunate but later brings a good result.
- **Hindi:** ऐसा दुख जो बाद में सुखद परिणाम दे.
- **Origin note:** Early printed examples are associated with eighteenth-century religious writing. James Hervey's 1746 hymn is often credited with popularizing the phrase, while some phrase-history accounts point to an earlier 1713 use by Edward Young. The safest summary is that the wording was in print by the eighteenth century; the first-ever spoken use and exact coinage are uncertain.
- **Example:** Missing that train was a blessing in disguise because I met my future business partner on the next one.
- **Memory hook:** Imagine a helpful gift hidden under an unexpected-looking disguise.

## 9. A blue-eyed boy
- **Meaning:** A person, often a young man, who is a particular favourite of someone in authority.
- **Hindi:** आँखों का तारा; बहुत प्रिय व्यक्ति.
- **Origin note:** This is chiefly British usage. P. G. Wodehouse’s 1919 novel *A Damsel in Distress* is often cited as an early literary example, and some references credit him with coining it; the exact origin is not certain. It does not necessarily mean the person literally has blue eyes.
- **Example:** The manager's blue-eyed boy was given the most visible assignment.
- **Memory hook:** Picture the teacher's favourite student getting special attention; the eye colour is part of the expression, not a requirement.

## 10. A bolt from the blue
- **Meaning:** A sudden, complete surprise, often an unpleasant one.
- **Hindi:** अचानक आई चौंकाने वाली घटना.
- **Origin note:** The image is lightning striking from a clear blue sky. A commonly cited early printed example is Thomas Carlyle's *The French Revolution* (1837). This is a documented image and early citation, not proof that Carlyle invented the phrase.
- **Example:** The sudden cancellation came as a bolt from the blue.
- **Memory hook:** A lightning bolt from a clear sky is startling because no storm seemed to be coming.
